\begin{abstract}
A law firm cannot send confidential case files to external language-model services, so every component of
this system runs on in-house hardware and starts from models we trained ourselves.  We pre-trained four
82--89M-parameter legal language models (decoder-only, encoder--decoder, prefix-LM, and an efficient decoder
with grouped-query and sliding-window attention) on a cleaned 1B-token sample of the official Indian legal
corpus, after an audit found validation leakage, duplicates and OCR noise in the starter data.  Each of the
six downstream tasks (entity recognition, rhetorical roles, judgment prediction, statute identification,
summarization and prior-case retrieval) fine-tunes these backbones with six to nine adaptation techniques,
including LoRMA and knowledge distillation.  Long documents are handled by chunking, condensation and, for
retrieval, citation-context windows.  Cleaning the corpus lowered A4 validation perplexity from 13.3 to 11.3
at an equal token budget, reading the last rather than the first chunks raised judgment-prediction macro-F1
from 0.754 to 0.785, and retrieving per citation raised micro-F1@7 from 0.32 to 0.43.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Description}
% ============================================================
A law firm that handles confidential matters needs language technology it can run on its own hardware: it
cannot send case files to commercial APIs, and it cannot fine-tune an outside foundation model whose training
data and licence it does not control.  The capstone therefore asks for a legal NLP stack built from a compact
language model pre-trained in house (50--300M parameters, no pretrained weights, no external corpora, official
data from \texttt{Exploration-Lab/CS-781-Capstone} only), which is then adapted to six downstream tasks drawn
from the IL-TUR benchmark \cite{joshi2024iltur}.  \autoref{tab:tasks} states each task.

\begin{table}[!t]\centering\footnotesize
\caption{Tasks (inputs, outputs and primary metrics as scored on Codabench).}\label{tab:tasks}
\scriptsize\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{clXl}\toprule
Ph. & Task & Input $\rightarrow$ output & Primary metric\\\midrule
1 & Legal LM & 512-word legal prompt $\rightarrow$ 128-word continuation & BERTScore F1\\
2 & L-NER & judgment text $\rightarrow$ character spans with one of 12 entity types & strict macro-F1\\
3 & Rhetorical roles & sentences of a judgment $\rightarrow$ one of 13 roles each (Corporate Law, IT) & macro-F1\\
4 & Judgment prediction & judgment (outcome removed) $\rightarrow$ allowed (1) / dismissed (0) & macro-F1\\
5 & Statute identification & case text (statute names masked) $\rightarrow$ set of statute ids (of 100) & macro-F1\\
6 & Summarization & judgment $\rightarrow$ abstractive summary & BERTScore F1\\
7 & Prior case retrieval & query judgment + candidate pool $\rightarrow$ 7 ranked case ids & micro-F1@7\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}\end{table}

Beyond the parameter cap and the data lock, three properties shaped every design decision.  First, most
documents are long (median 2,469 tokens in Phase~4, 3,123 words in Phase~6 and 4,508 words in Phase~7) while
the Phase-1 models have a 1,024-token context.  Second, the metrics are strict: NER counts only exact character spans, Phases 2--5 average F1 over
classes so rare labels count fully, and summaries are scored against a single reference.  Third, compute was one
GPU shared with other users.

We investigated whether cleaning the pre-training corpus matters more than architecture, how the four
backbones transfer to each task, which adaptation techniques keep full-fine-tuning quality with few trainable
parameters, how to represent documents four to eight times longer than the context, and how decoding and
thresholding choices move the metrics.  We built and submitted every phase end to end; we did
not pipeline the phases, train task-specific tokenizers, or run a human legal evaluation.

\paragraph{Integrity findings.}  Each of the following changed the protocol.  The Phase-1 starter split
contained validation documents copied into training, so we rebuilt a validation set that shares no text with
either corpus.  In Phase~4, \texttt{test.json} carries
labels and all 1,517 test documents occur, labelled, in \texttt{dev.json}; we never read those labels and
selected models only on the 1,988 development documents not in the test set.  In Phase~5, development texts that also
occur in the test file (96) or in training (118) were removed, and 435 duplicate training texts merged.  In Phase~7, every query occurs in its own candidate pool
(never as its own gold case), and a pool is essentially the union of its queries' gold cases: 99.9\% (train)
and 100\% (dev) of pool cases that are not themselves queries are gold for some query of the split.

\subsection{Preprocessing decisions}
All models share one byte-level BPE tokenizer \cite{sennrich2016bpe} (32,000 tokens, trained on legal
text), so Devanagari names, section symbols and OCR debris never become unknown tokens.  Phase-1 cleaning
normalised characters and markup, repaired word stutter, filtered short or non-textual documents and removed
exact and MinHash near-duplicates.  For NER, every span is mapped from characters to BPE tokens through the
tokenizer's offset table; runs of punctuation are split before encoding because they otherwise merge with
entity boundaries (the span-to-tag-to-span round trip recovers 99.45\% of gold spans instead of 98.66\%).
Phase~3 uses the provided sentence lists as they are.  Long documents are handled per task: eight 512-token
chunks (last eight in Phase~4, evenly spread in Phase~5), a condensed source of 1,280--8,192 tokens in Phase~6
(the opening of the case plus the sentences closest to the document's TF-IDF centroid), and citation windows of
$\pm60$ words plus 512-token candidate chunks in Phase~7.

\section{Approach and Model Evolution}
% ============================================================
\subsection{Phase-1 foundation model}
Four architectures share tokenizer, data, schedule and evaluation, so only the architecture differs
(\autoref{tab:arch}); parameter counts are summed over the instantiated models (tied embeddings).  All are trained with next-token
cross-entropy on 1,024-token windows; A2 learns to continue a random-length prefix and A3 reads a random
prefix bidirectionally.  The first runs (R0) used the starter sample of about 200M tokens.  After the audit
described above, we retrained on the cleaned corpus at the same budget (R1) and continued to 1B tokens (R2).
The submission decoder samples with temperature 0.8, top-$k$ 50, top-$p$ 0.95 and repetition penalty 1.1 over
the last 256 tokens \cite{keskar2019ctrl}, and forbids end-of-text before 128 words.

\subsection{Transfer protocol (Phases 2--7)}
Every downstream model starts from a Phase-1 checkpoint (cleaned R2 from Phase~3 on; Phase~2 also used R0)
and is fine-tuned on task data only (\autoref{fig:system}).  A shared wrapper exposes the backbones as
encoders: A1 and A4 keep their causal mask (better than bidirectional in Phase~3), A3 reads a bidirectional
prefix, and A2 contributes decoder states because its Phase-1 encoder output is nearly collapsed.
Adaptation techniques were implemented once and reused: full and partial fine-tuning, gradual unfreezing
\cite{howard2018ulmfit}, BitFit \cite{benzaken2022bitfit}, LoRA \cite{hu2022lora}, LoRMA
\cite{bihany2025lorma}, QLoRA with NF4 weights \cite{dettmers2023qlora}, AdaLoRA \cite{zhang2023adalora},
Houlsby adapters \cite{houlsby2019adapters}, IA$^3$ \cite{liu2022ia3}, prefix \cite{li2021prefix} and prompt
tuning \cite{lester2021prompt}, and knowledge distillation \cite{hinton2015kd} into students that keep a subset
of the teacher's blocks.  Each phase used at least six of them across all four architectures.

\subsection{Phase 2 --- Legal NER}
NER is token classification followed by exact offset reconstruction: gold spans become BIO tags over BPE
tokens, each backbone tags overlapping 512-token windows (stride 128, each token keeps the window with the most
context), and a Viterbi decoder that only allows valid BIO transitions turns tag scores back into character
spans.  Six experiments covered all four architectures.

\subsection{Phase 3 --- Rhetorical role prediction}
Each document is encoded in windows of neighbouring sentences; every sentence is mean-pooled, a BiLSTM with
relative-position and domain embeddings reads the document, and a sentence-level CRF \cite{lafferty2001crf}
decodes the role sequence, matching the long label runs in \autoref{tab:data}.  Corporate Law and IT were
trained jointly with a domain embedding, under five-fold document-level cross-validation over the 90 labelled
documents.  A logit-adjusted loss gives rare roles more weight.

\subsection{Phase 4 --- Court judgment prediction}
Eight 512-token chunks are encoded, mean-pooled, read by a BiGRU and pooled by attention \cite{yang2016han}.
The loss is class-weighted and the decision threshold is tuned on validation, because training is 59\% label 0
while validation and test are balanced; macro-F1, not accuracy, makes both outcomes count equally.

\subsection{Phase 5 --- Legal statute identification}
The same chunk encoder feeds a BiGRU and one attention query per statute, so each statute reads the part of the
judgment that discusses it, followed by 100 independent sigmoids.  Binary cross-entropy uses positive weights
$\sqrt{n_\text{neg}/n_\text{pos}}$ (capped at 30) for rare statutes, and a threshold is tuned per statute on
validation.  Later rounds distilled 6-block students from ensembles of earlier runs, and MLP and per-statute
LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} stackers were trained out-of-fold on validation over all runs' logits,
optionally with TF-IDF models (a hybrid without pretrained weights).

\subsection{Phase 6 --- Abstractive summarization}
The decoder-only models read [prompt, condensed source, ``\texttt{Summary:}''] and are trained on the summary
tokens; A2 uses fusion-in-decoder \cite{izacard2021fid} over four 1,024-token chunks.  A1's position table was
linearly interpolated \cite{chen2023pi} (ALiBi and RoPE extrapolate) so that sources could grow from 1,280 to
8,192 tokens, each run starting from the previous shorter-source checkpoint.  Decoding is greedy with repetition penalty 1.15 and no repeated 3-grams.
The final step selects, per document, the candidate summary that agrees most with the other candidates
(minimum-Bayes-risk selection \cite{kumar2004mbr} with ROUGE-1 + ROUGE-2 agreement).  No preference
optimisation or other post-training was used.

\subsection{Phase 7 --- Prior case retrieval}
The text around each masked citation describes one cited case, so retrieval works per citation window.  The
lexical stage issues one BM25 \cite{robertson2009bm25} query per window, penalises candidates dated after the
query, and normalises each candidate's scores across the split's queries and windows, because every pool case
is cited by some query.  The neural stage, a bi-encoder on a Phase-1 backbone, scores a window by its best-matching
512-token candidate chunk and is trained with multi-positive InfoNCE \cite{oord2018cpc} and BM25 hard negatives.  A LightGBM lambdarank model re-ranks the top 100
lexical candidates, and the mean score of 13 trained retrievers is added to its output.

\section{Experiments and Implementation Details}

\subsection{Compute and training protocol}
All runs used one NVIDIA H200 shared with other users (two to four concurrent runs).  Phase-1 R2 processed
803M tokens in 0.8--1.1 hours per architecture; downstream runs took 2 minutes to 3.5 hours.  All used AdamW,
warm-up and cosine decay, clipping at 1.0 and bf16, kept their best state and were resumable.  Selection used
validation loss (P1, P6) or the official metric on the held-out split of \autoref{sec:localeval} (P2--P5, P7).

\subsection{Local evaluation}\label{sec:localeval}
Held-out splits use public data only: a leak-free Phase-1 validation set, 10 of the 70 NER training documents
(confirmed on three splits), five-fold document-level cross-validation (P3), development documents not in the test
set (P4), de-duplicated development data with cross-fitted thresholds (P5), 300 drawn training documents (P6), and
the official dev split (P7).  A script checked every submission's schema
before zipping (no missing or extra ids, one label per sentence, unique statute ids, non-empty summaries,
7 distinct pool ids per query that never include the query).  Local and Codabench scores can differ because some splits
are small and the local BERTScore uses roberta-large, not the organisers' ``legal BERTScore''.

\paragraph{Which system won, and why.}  The winners differ by phase, but the reasons repeat.  In
\textbf{Phase~1}, A1, A3 and A4 finish within 0.0006 of each other on Codabench, while A2 trails (0.8184): with
the same data and budget, the decoder-style models are interchangeable, and the encoder--decoder pays for
splitting its capacity between two stacks (its encoder output later turned out to be nearly constant).  In
\textbf{Phase~2}, A4 with LoRMA won with only 2.2M trained parameters.  With 70 training documents, updating a
small multiplicative correction preserves what pre-training learned better than rewriting all weights, and
the constrained decoder mattered even more than the backbone (argmax 0.373 vs.\ Viterbi 0.486).  In
\textbf{Phase~3}, the winner (A1 LoRA with 1,024-token windows) is the variant that sees the most neighbouring
sentences; adding context was the single largest change we measured (0.361 to 0.455), because roles are defined
by where a sentence sits in the argument.  In \textbf{Phase~4}, the stacker won because its members read
different parts of the judgment (last, first, spread chunks) and therefore make different mistakes, and the
end of the judgment, where the outcome is argued, carries most of the signal (0.785 vs.\ 0.754).  In
\textbf{Phase~5}, the hybrid stack beat the Phase-1-only stack (0.462 vs.\ 0.423 on Codabench): statute names are
masked, but TF-IDF models still pick up exact phrases that accompany a statute (offence wording, procedural
terms) across the whole document, which eight 512-token chunks cannot all cover, so the two families err on
different documents.  In \textbf{Phase~6}, longer sources won (1,280 to 2,560 tokens: +0.0015), since a
summary cannot mention what the model never read, and beam search helped more on Codabench (0.8558 vs.\ 0.8520
greedy) than locally.  In \textbf{Phase~7}, most of the score comes from matching each citation separately and
from normalising across the pool (0.324 to 0.483 with no learning); the Phase-1 retrievers add about 0.01--0.02
on top by recognising paraphrased legal reasoning that word overlap misses.

% ============================================================
\section{Error Analysis and Discussion}
% ============================================================
\subsection{Phase-wise failure modes}
Every phase fails in a way that tells us something about legal text.  In \textbf{NER}, the problem is mostly
silence rather than confusion: 37.9\% of gold entities are never predicted, against 8.0\% with a wrong boundary
and 5.6\% with a wrong type.  Strict span matching turns any boundary slip into a miss, and the commonest type
error, appellant tagged as respondent (35 cases), is one that a 512-token window cannot resolve, because who is
the appellant is settled pages earlier.  In \textbf{rhetorical roles}, the model mixes up Ratio and
PrecedentRelied (548 and 539 sentences) and Fact and Ratio (499).  These sentences use the same vocabulary; what
differs is \emph{whose} reasoning it is, a discourse cue the sentence encoder only partly sees.  Roles with three
training sentences are simply never learned.  In \textbf{judgment prediction}, errors are fairly balanced (176
false positives, 250 false negatives), and reading the last chunks instead of the first (0.785 vs.\ 0.754)
supports the idea that the outcome is argued near the end.  In \textbf{statute identification}, the most common
error is telling: a missed statute is replaced by the most frequent one, so when evidence is weak the model falls
back on its prior, and rare statutes suffer (mean F1 0.32 for the rarest fifth, 0.56 for the most frequent).  In
\textbf{summarization}, scores fall as judgments get longer (correlation $-0.40$): condensation has to drop more,
and whatever it drops cannot appear in the summary.  In \textbf{retrieval}, 8 of 118 dev queries get no correct
case, and queries citing only one to three cases are capped by the fixed list of seven (precision 0.21).

\subsection{What the metrics do not measure}
None of these numbers checks legal correctness.  A summary can score a high BERTScore while stating the wrong
holding, because embedding similarity rewards shared vocabulary, and only its first $\approx$400 words are
compared at all.  Strict span-F1 counts an entity as wrong when one character is off, which is harsher than a
lawyer would be, while micro-F1@7 forces seven answers even when a case cites one.  For a firm, this means every
output should be treated as a draft with a confidence attached, never as a final answer.

\subsection{Limitations and what you would do next}
We trained on one shared GPU, so most runs have a single seed, and our validation sets are small (118 queries,
300 summaries), which makes differences below about 0.015 noise.  The Phase-1 models never saw more than 1,024
tokens, yet Phases 4--7 run them on documents several times longer.  We had no human legal evaluation, and two
of our best systems (Phases 5 and 7) lean on lexical models.  With more time we would continue pre-training the
cleaned A4 model at 4,096 tokens, resolve party roles at the document level for NER, and pre-train the retriever
on citation contexts mined from the Phase-1 corpus itself.

\subsection{Surprising findings}
Three results went against our expectations.  The cleaned corpus lowered perplexity everywhere, yet NER got
slightly worse with the new backbone (0.488 to 0.467).  In Phase~7, the largest gains came from the structure of
the data (one query per citation, normalising scores across the pool), not from the neural model.  And local
and leaderboard rankings did not always agree: in Phase~3 a single model beat our ensembles on Codabench.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================
We delivered what the brief asked for: four legal language models trained from scratch and six task systems
built on them, all running on in-house hardware with no outside data or services.  What moved the metrics was
mostly how we handled the documents (cleaning the corpus, reading the end of a judgment, feeding longer
sources, retrieving per citation) and combining models, while the choice among architectures and adaptation
methods mattered less, with LoRMA and partial fine-tuning close to full fine-tuning.  The main lesson is that a
small model gains more from understanding the shape of legal text than from extra parameters.  A sceptical
partner should still not trust the summaries without reading the judgment, should expect the retriever to miss
about half of the cited cases, and should know that NER misses roughly a third of entities.
